# Eight vetoes: adapted diagnostic reference

Adapted from the author's framework for a read-only GitHub page audit. The public skill contains these operational adaptations, not the unpublished book chapters. Apply the lenses together without scoring them or forcing a finding under each.

## 1. Demand — “I don't need it.”

Ask whether the page establishes a reason for its intended reader to care. Look for a recognisable situation, task or desire and a meaningful consequence: time, inconvenience, risk, opportunity, learning, creativity or enjoyment. A functioning implementation and a list of features are not evidence of demand. A problem can be real but unimportant to a particular audience.

Feedback should identify relevance left unstated. “The page has not established why this audience would want it” is defensible; “there is no market” is not. Do not require a painful business problem from a hobby or creative project. Customer research and product changes are outside the audit.

## 2. Visibility — “I've never heard of it.”

Inspect whether the page and its metadata support relevant discovery and a memorable association between the project's name and purpose. Prefer natural task/category language to broad technology slogans. Builder interest, stars and traffic are not evidence that the right users are being reached.

The README alone cannot diagnose exposure or awareness. Apply the separate Discoverability checks. Do not recommend external launch channels, repeated posting, campaigns or keyword stuffing.

## 3. Clarity — “I don't get it.”

Ask whether an unfamiliar intended reader can explain what the project does, whom it helps, and the useful result. Identify assumptions that rely on the creator's background knowledge. Connect key features to relevant benefits while preserving specifications, constraints and integrations needed by specialist readers.

Judge ordering, examples, screenshots, output and next steps. Technical terms and AI labels must contribute meaning. Do not impose a five-second comprehension rule, generic benefit slogans, or a universal opening formula. Understanding and wanting are different outcomes.

## 4. Preference — “Why should I choose yours?”

Consider the alternative the reader might actually use: a library, service, script, manual workflow, or doing nothing. Look for a specific supported advantage relevant to that audience. Breadth matters when it completes a useful workflow; novelty and feature count alone do not establish preference.

Positioning must be supported by demonstrated capabilities. Do not invent competitors' limitations or claim uniqueness without evidence. Candid fit and trade-offs can be more useful than a checkmark matrix. A small open-source utility does not need a permanent competitive moat.

## 5. Confidence — “I don't trust it.”

Ask which uncertainties matter given the reliance requested. A local formatting helper and a tool that receives production credentials need different explanations. Inspect claim-specific evidence, realistic demonstrations, maturity, limitations, relevant data/permission explanations, and documented routes for help.

Public code, download counts and build badges do not establish security, satisfaction or operational reliability. Missing explanation is not proof of unsafe behaviour. Do not fabricate endorsements, imply certification, or demand enterprise support from volunteer maintainers. The audit reviews communication, not the actual security controls.

## 6. Value — “It's not worth my time.”

Ask whether the expected result appears worth evaluation, learning, setup, workflow change and upkeep. Free software still requires effort. Value depends on frequency and circumstances, and may be educational, creative or experiential. Paid APIs and hosting matter when actually required, but pricing is not the default focus.

Check whether examples let readers judge the return on effort before committing. Do not invent setup-time or savings estimates. Necessary complexity can be worthwhile; concealment does not improve value. Distinguish a benefit not communicated from a benefit not established.

## 7. Friction — “It's too much hassle.”

Trace the documented route from interest to first meaningful result, not merely installation. Look for buried compatibility details, unexplained prerequisites, missing transitions, unnecessary choices, absent configuration, and output without success criteria. If supported, a sample path can reduce uncertainty.

Distinguish incomplete instructions from a mandatory product constraint. Explain adoption work honestly; do not recommend non-existent demos or migration tools. Do not run install commands or assert they work. Friction concerns reaching the benefit; value concerns whether that effort is justified.

## 8. Experience — “It didn't deliver.”

Assess expectations, not actual runtime delivery. Compare the outcome implied by headings and demonstrations with the narrower capability, manual work, conditions and limits documented elsewhere. “Automatically fixes builds” may overstate “suggests patches for human review.”

Examples should show a meaningful result and disclose representative conditions where necessary. Put consequential qualifications close to the promise. Infrequent use is not automatically failure; a one-off utility can complete its job once. Wording cannot fix an unreliable product, and absent evidence does not prove failure. Product testing, retention analysis and operational changes are outside scope.

## Applying the framework

The four Cs organise findings; the vetoes guide diagnosis. One issue can affect multiple lenses. Combine overlaps, prioritise consequences, and distinguish observations, inferences, unknowns and items that do not apply. Do not expose an eight-part checklist unless the user specifically requests that form.
